How to reduce video file size

Every "reduce video size" trick boils down to one equation: size ≈ bitrate × duration. Change one of the three inputs and the size follows.

Last updated: October 2026

The one equation

A video file is mostly its video bitrate times its duration, plus a smaller audio share. So there are exactly three levers: lower the bitrate, lower the resolution (which lets a lower bitrate still look good), or shorten the clip.

Any tool that promises otherwise is selling something. "AI compression" still moves one of these three numbers.

Lever 1: Resolution

Dropping 1080p to 720p roughly halves the pixels the encoder must describe — so the same bitrate looks much better, or a lower bitrate looks the same. This is why good compressors step down the resolution ladder (1080p → 720p → 480p) instead of starving a 4K frame with a tiny bitrate.

For phone screens and chat apps, 720p is usually indistinguishable from 1080p. For a TV, it isn't — pick the ladder rung for the screen it will be watched on.

Lever 2: Bitrate — exact beats preset

Most tools offer Low / Medium / High and hope the output fits. That is backwards: the platform cares about megabytes, not your quality setting. Start from the number that matters — 20 MB, 50 MB, any exact size — and solve for the bitrate that fits it.

Lever 3: Length

The most underused lever. Cutting 30 dead seconds off a clip saves more size than any quality tweak, with zero quality loss. Trim first, compress second.

And one warning: don't re-compress an already-compressed file repeatedly. Each pass throws away detail you can't get back. Compress once, from the original.

FAQ

Does compressing reduce quality?

It depends on the math. A 30-second clip at 20 MB looks great; a 20-minute clip at 20 MB cannot. If the target is unrealistic for the length, trim the clip or pick a bigger target.

What's the best format for small files?

MP4 with H.264 video and AAC audio — the universal choice. Newer codecs (H.265, AV1) compress better but don't play everywhere yet.
